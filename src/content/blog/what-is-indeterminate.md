Werner Heisenberg died fifty years ago, and on 30 October I spoke about him at a round table organised by Nuovo SEFIR. There are many things one can say about Heisenberg: matrix mechanics at twenty-three, the uncertainty paper at twenty-five, the early quantum field theory with Pauli, the proton-neutron model of the nucleus, the Nobel Prize for 1932. I chose to say one thing, and to try to say it well.

The one thing is a question. We all learned that, according to Heisenberg, position and momentum cannot be known together. Put that way, the claim concerns the limits of our knowledge. Read the texts, however, and three different claims appear, and the difference between them is a philosophical problem in its own right. When we say that position and momentum are not determined together, what exactly is missing? It may be our knowledge; it may be the possibility of saying anything about reality beyond what we measure; or it may be definite values in reality itself.

## 1925: giving up the orbit

The question has its roots in the paper Heisenberg wrote on Helgoland in June 1925. Bohr's atom described electrons moving on orbits that nobody had ever observed; what one observes of an atom is the light it emits. Heisenberg decided to keep only that. The abstract announces a quantum mechanics founded exclusively on relations between quantities that are observable in principle, and the second page draws the consequence: it seems wiser to give up altogether the hope of observing the position and period of the electron.

Carlo Rovelli, in *Helgoland*, describes the move well: Heisenberg keeps the known forces and the known equations, and changes the way of thinking about the electron. In place of a position that varies in time he writes tables of numbers, one for each transition between states. When two such tables are multiplied, the order matters. Heisenberg noted this as a difficulty in half a line; within weeks Born recognised matrices, and the difficulty became the core of the theory. The first contribution is already conceptual. Heisenberg decided which quantities have physical meaning and let the mathematics draw the consequences.

## 1927: the microscope

Two years later Heisenberg asked what his formalism means in a simple experiment. The answer is the famous gamma-ray microscope. To locate an electron precisely one needs light of very short wavelength, and such light gives the electron a sudden kick. The more precise the position, the larger the change of momentum. This is the story everyone knows.

Two remarks complicate it. The rigorous inequality proved by Kennard a few months later says nothing about kicks: it constrains the statistical spread of position and momentum in ensembles prepared in the same way. And in his 1929 Chicago lectures Heisenberg himself observed that the relation does not hold for the past, since after the collision both values can be reconstructed. What it forbids is prediction. From the beginning, the formula and the story do not coincide.

## Three readings

The 1927 paper contains three answers to the question, which I call epistemic, agnostic and realist.

The **epistemic** reading concerns what we can know. The electron has a position and a momentum, and measuring one disturbs the other. This is the reading suggested by the microscope, it is the one that survives in common sense, and it is still the reading of the Bohmians, for whom every particle always has a definite position. Bohr objected at once: in that experiment position and momentum cannot be defined together, and the epistemic reading presupposes precisely a hidden value that the theory, as he and Heisenberg formulated it, does not supply.

The **agnostic** reading concerns what can be said. Its starting point is semantic: an expression such as "the position of the electron" has meaning only if one specifies an experiment that measures it. Applied to the orbit of the electron in the hydrogen atom, the conclusion is that a single light quantum suitable for observing it would knock the electron out of its orbit, so the word has no reasonable sense there. On this reading, nothing is affirmed and nothing is denied about the electron beyond measurement. The question is suspended. Its cost is the identification of meaning with measurability, a thesis with many supporters in 1927 and few today.

The **realist** reading concerns what there is. In the same paper Heisenberg writes that the path comes into being only through our observing it. The indeterminacy is placed in nature. The realism here is realism about indeterminacy: before measurement, what is real is a property without a definite value. The epistemic reading is realist too, in its own way, since it takes the hidden values to exist. The difference lies in where the indeterminacy is located.

The three readings are not successive stages. They sit a few pages apart in the same paper, and Heisenberg moves between them without comment, because he took measuring a quantity to give it both meaning and reality. Rovelli reports Pauli's verdict on his friend: all intuition, little care for stating assumptions clearly. The paper nonetheless ends with a firm choice. Of the strong causal law, "if we know the present exactly, we can calculate the future", Heisenberg says that the premise is false, and he calls the idea of a hidden causal world behind the statistical one fruitless.

## Potentia

The realist reading receives its fullest form thirty years later, in the Gifford Lectures published as *Physics and Philosophy*. The probability wave, Heisenberg writes, means "a tendency for something", a quantitative version of the Aristotelian *potentia*: something between the idea of an event and the actual event. An indeterminate property is neither missing nor unknown. It is a property in potency, a real tendency of the electron to be found here rather than there, actualised in measurement. In today's vocabulary one would speak of dispositions or propensities.

The seed is already in 1927, where every perception is described as a selection from a plenitude of possibilities. Even in 1958, however, Heisenberg does not decide completely: the probability function also contains our incomplete knowledge, and its sudden change at measurement is a change in what we know. The realist and the epistemic readings coexist until the end.

## Who observes?

None of this makes consciousness the creator of reality, and Heisenberg says so explicitly. The transition from the possible to the actual takes place as soon as the object interacts with the measuring device and, through it, with the rest of the world. His 1935 manuscript replying to Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen states it sharply: the blackening of a photographic plate is an objective fact, whoever looks at it and whenever. The same manuscript shows that the cut between the quantum system and the classical apparatus can be moved toward the observer without changing the predictions, while it cannot be removed. Heisenberg's observer is the classical side of that cut.

## What remains open

The uncertainty relations are usually presented as a limit on what we can know. In Heisenberg's texts they are a question about what is indeterminate, answered three times, in three ways that he never fully separated. The debate is still alive: physicists argue about rigorous error-disturbance relations alongside Kennard's inequality, and philosophers about metaphysical indeterminacy and unsharp properties. The question Heisenberg left is what is real between one observation and the next. His answer was that possibilities and tendencies belong to nature as much as facts do.
