At the [Bridging Worlds](/blog/bridging-worlds/) conference in Castel Gandolfo, one of the two lines of work was the attempt to map and measure what faith-based actors contribute to development. In the previous note I looked at the logic of the statements this research makes. Here I want to ask a prior question, which the natural sciences faced long ago: what exactly can be measured when the object is something like communion, trust or the common good, and what can the history of physics teach us about it?

## Galileo's choice

In 1612, in the third of his letters on sunspots, Galileo drew a line that would define modern science. Either we try, by speculation, "to penetrate the true and intrinsic essence of natural substances", or we content ourselves "with coming to know some of their affections".[^galileo] He thought the first task hopeless for the moment, and chose the second: place, motion, shape, size, the properties that can be observed and measured. Physics has followed this choice ever since. We do not know what gravity *is* in itself, but we measure its effects with extraordinary precision.

The analogy with our case is direct. No one can measure communion, faith or flourishing in themselves. What can be measured are their affections: the practices they give rise to, the effects they produce, the traces they leave in the life of a community. <mark>This is not a defeat. It is the same renunciation that made physics possible.</mark> The usual objection that the most important things cannot be measured is true, but it is true of mass and temperature as well, and it did not stop physics from measuring them.

## Where the analogy breaks

The analogy, however, breaks at two points, and it is worth seeing exactly where.

The first concerns the bridge between what is measured and what it is supposed to indicate. When a column of mercury rises, a well-tested theory tells us why its height says something about temperature. Between the observable effects of a faith-based initiative and the relational good that is supposed to produce them, no comparable theory exists. <mark>The same effects (cooperation, sharing, social impact) can come from communion, but also from strategy, convenience or good marketing.</mark> In physics the affections are connected to the underlying quantity by a law. Here the connection has to be argued for case by case.

The second concerns the effect of measurement on what is measured. In physics, too, measuring means interacting. As I recalled in [a note on Heisenberg](/blog/what-is-indeterminate/), in quantum theory a measurement is a physical interaction with an apparatus, and what is measured depends on it. But the electron has no interest in the outcome. Communities, firms and organisations do. Once they know how they will be assessed, they respond, and sociologists have documented how rankings reshape the institutions they rank.[^reactivity] <mark>The interaction between measure and measured is no longer physical but strategic</mark>, and this changes everything that follows.

## The circle

There is a further difficulty, which philosophers of science know well. To choose the indicators of communion we need to know what communion is; to know whether the indicators work we need to be able to measure communion already. This is a version of what is called the problem of coordination, and physics met it too.[^coordination]

Hasok Chang has shown how the circle was broken in the case of temperature.[^chang] At the beginning there was a vague, graded concept tied to sensation. To build a reliable thermometer one needed fixed points, but water does not boil at a fixed temperature unless conditions are carefully controlled; to check a thermometer one needed to know the temperature, which was precisely what the thermometer was meant to measure. The way out was what Chang calls epistemic iteration: concepts and instruments were refined together, over generations, by comparing instruments built on different principles and correcting each against the others.

The lesson is encouraging. The mapping of faith-based development is at something like the stage of early thermometry, and this is the normal beginning of measurement rather than a sign of failure. But iteration only works if something stands outside the circle and can correct it. For temperature, it was the comparison between instruments that rested on different physical principles. In our case, it can only be sources of evidence that do not depend on the indicators themselves: cases followed over many years, independent verification, and the judgement of those who live the experience being measured.

## The risk of washing

The two disanalogies together explain a risk that the recent history of finance has made familiar. Environmental, social and governance ratings were designed to make sustainability visible to investors, and they did. But because what they measured could be produced without the substance it was meant to signal, they also made room for greenwashing, i.e. the production of measurable sustainability without much sustainability behind it. The different agencies often disagree about the same firm,[^esg] and the European Union has recently had to regulate the rating activity itself.[^eu]

The same could happen here. <mark>The separability of effects from essence, which in physics is a virtue, is precisely what makes counterfeiting possible when the measured have an interest in the result.</mark> One might call the danger faith-washing: the production of the measurable affections of a faith-based contribution without the relational good they are supposed to signal. This is not an accusation against anyone. It is a structural possibility, and it follows from the very choice that makes measurement possible.

## A condition, and a task

The conclusion I draw is not that faith-based development should not be measured. It is a condition. <mark>Galileo's choice to measure affections rather than essences is legitimate here too, but it works only if the link between affections and essence is built and tested, rather than assumed.</mark> In physics that link is a theory. Here it has to be a body of work: conceptual analysis of what relational and religious goods are, comparison between independent sources of evidence, long-term study of cases, and a clear account of what each indicator can and cannot show.

This is, in the end, academic work, and of a kind that no single discipline can do alone. It needs philosophers of measurement and social scientists, economists and theologians, and the practitioners whose experience is being measured. The Bridging Worlds conference has opened the question. Keeping it open, and answering it well, is a task that will take years, and it is worth taking up seriously.
