At the end of the Consulus Global School in London, I was asked what had been the most meaningful moment of the week. I did not name a talk. What stayed with me was something I experienced rather than something I heard: the composition of the room. Over three days — in a church in the City, at Lambeth Palace, at the Faith Centre of the London School of Economics — about a hundred and forty people from some thirty countries worked on the same questions: how faith-based capital can be directed towards social impact, how the Economy of Communion can be measured, and how different religious traditions can work together on it. Among others, there were investors who manage family wealth in Asia, a former head of an Islamic bank, the director of a Buddhist community, bishops of different churches, consultants, entrepreneurs, and a few academics.

I want to take that answer a little further, because I think it says something about the university that goes beyond a single event.

## The sudoku problem

Much academic work today happens in small circles. A community of specialists shares a vocabulary, a set of canonical problems, and a set of accepted moves. Within that circle, progress is real and often technically impressive. But from the outside, and sometimes from the inside too, part of the debate can come to resemble a sudoku: a well-defined puzzle with fixed rules, where the challenge is to fill in the remaining cells correctly. Solving it requires skill. Whether it was worth solving is a question the puzzle itself does not easily raise. Thomas Kuhn described much of normal science as puzzle-solving, and he did not mean it as a criticism.[^1]

This is not an argument against specialisation. Precision is not a minor virtue, and some ideas worked out in closed circles turned out to matter long after, for reasons no one outside could have foreseen: number theory, which G. H. Hardy valued precisely for its uselessness, now underpins modern cryptography.[^2] The risk is a narrower one: when questions come only from within the circle, and for long enough, the circle can stop being exposed to the questions that would unsettle it.

## What plurality can produce

A space like the one in London does not address this by bringing in "the public" as an audience for academic results. It works differently. When an investor, a scholar of interreligious dialogue, and a philosopher of science discuss what it would mean for capital to serve the common good, none of them can fully rely on the implicit agreements of their own field. Each is pushed to state assumptions that normally remain unspoken, and each may hear objections that would not arise among peers. Peter Galison has called settings of this kind trading zones: places where groups with different languages build local means of exchange without first having to agree on everything.[^3]

Two examples from the week. The first is the Multi-Faith Economy Index, launched at Lambeth Palace: an attempt to measure how far financial institutions act on the faith commitments they profess. The question comes from people whose work is to allocate capital. For a philosopher it touches a question that the philosophy of measurement has long discussed, and that the debate on ESG ratings has recently made concrete: what it means to measure fidelity to a value, and what such a measurement can and cannot carry.[^4] The second is the panel on AI and religions in which I took part. The moderator asked what religious communities can contribute that governments, businesses and technologists cannot achieve alone. In an academic seminar I would probably not have framed the question that way, and answering it pushed me to say more precisely what I mean by formation.

A space of this kind asks something of everyone: a shared object, some time, and a willingness to have one's own question changed. And everyone can take something away from it, not only the academics. In this sense such spaces seem to me to matter epistemically, and not only socially. They help to change the questions. A question that comes from someone whose job is to decide where capital goes, or from someone who accompanies a religious community through rapid change, is not necessarily a naive version of an academic question. It is sometimes a different question, and one the academic would not have formulated alone.

## Transdisciplinarity

Transdisciplinarity is often taken as a stronger synonym for interdisciplinarity: several disciplines collaborating on a common object. In the tradition of Basarab Nicolescu it means something more demanding, with an ontological and logical core — levels of reality, the logic of the included middle, complexity — and a concern for what lies *between*, *across* and *beyond* the disciplines.[^5] I have argued elsewhere that, understood in this way, it involves the knower and not only what is known, and that this is part of what keeps it a human task in the age of generative AI.[^6] An experience like the one in London brings one of its consequences into view: if there are levels of reality and experience that no single discipline reaches, then those who live a problem may see aspects of it that the disciplines miss.

## A tension worth naming

The academic system does not easily reward this kind of engagement. Evaluation is built around publications in specialised venues, read by specialised peers. Time spent in plural spaces tends to be regarded as time taken away from research, or as an institutional duty — the university's "third mission", placed alongside teaching and research rather than within them.

I am not sure this arrangement is simply wrong; specialised work needs protected time, and it should not have to justify itself by its exposure to outside questions. But for some kinds of research, at least, that exposure seems to be more than an addition once the real work is done. It may be one of the ways research keeps from turning into a sudoku.
