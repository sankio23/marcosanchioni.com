String theorists often say that their theory contains a shortest length: below a certain scale, the string length $\ell_s$, distances are said to stop making sense. The claim is usually traced to a property of the theory called T-duality. This note explains T-duality with as little machinery as possible and then asks what it shows about length. My suggestion is that it does not show that small distances are missing. It shows that, at these scales, the size of space depends on what one measures it with.

## A circle and two ways to use it

Suppose that one direction of space is not a line but a circle of radius $R$, like the surface of a very long garden hose seen from far away. Along the other directions, particles and strings move as usual. What matters is what they can do with the circle.

A closed string has two options. The first is to travel around the circle, like a bead sliding along a ring. In quantum mechanics a particle confined to a circle behaves like a wave that must fit a whole number of times around it. The smaller the circle, the shorter the wave, and the shorter the wave, the more energy it carries. The energy of the bead therefore grows as the circle shrinks: it goes like $1/R$.

The second option is not available to a point particle. A closed string can wrap around the circle, like a rubber band stretched around a tin can. A stretched string has a tension, and its energy is tension times length. The larger the circle, the longer the band, and the more energy it carries. The energy of the band therefore grows with the circle: it goes like $R$.

Physicists call the first kind of state a *momentum* state and the second a *winding* state. Each is labelled by a whole number: $n$ counts how many wavelengths fit around the circle, $w$ how many times the string wraps around it.

## The symmetry

Taking both contributions into account, the mass of a string state on the circle is

$$
M^2 = \left(\frac{n}{R}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{w R}{\ell_s^2}\right)^2 + \text{(vibrations of the string)}.
$$

Now exchange $n$ and $w$, so that every bead becomes a band and every band a bead, and at the same time replace $R$ with $\ell_s^2/R$. The list of possible masses does not change. A large circle and a small circle, with beads and bands exchanged, give exactly the same particles, and the ways in which the strings interact also coincide.[^1]

This is T-duality. A string theory on a circle of radius $R$ is physically indistinguishable from a string theory on a circle of radius $\ell_s^2/R$. The two descriptions coincide when $R = \ell_s$, the self-dual radius.

## The usual conclusion

The standard argument runs as follows. Try to shrink the circle below $\ell_s$: the result is a theory identical to one with a circle larger than $\ell_s$. Shrinking further only takes one back up. There is therefore no physically distinct situation corresponding to a circle smaller than the string length, and $\ell_s$ is the shortest length.

What follows about space has been read in three ways. On an epistemic reading, small circles exist, but we cannot find out about them, because the probes needed to see them are too heavy to produce.[^2] On an eliminative reading, small distances are simply not there; this is how Witten put it.[^3] On an indeterminist reading, the two descriptions disagree about the radius while agreeing on everything physical, so there is no fact about what the radius is, and the definite space we observe must emerge from something that is not spatial in the ordinary sense.[^4]

## Two rulers

Return to the bead and the band. Each is a way of probing the circle, and each gives a definite answer.

A bead lives in the ordinary space of the circle. Its wave has to fit around a circle of radius $R$, and $R$ can be read off its energy levels. A band behaves, mathematically, like a wave living on a different circle, of radius $\ell_s^2/R$, which physicists sometimes call the winding space.[^5] From the band's energy levels one reads off $\ell_s^2/R$.

There are two rulers, then, and they give reciprocal answers. Neither is wrong: the bead-ruler reports $R$, the band-ruler reports $\ell_s^2/R$. What T-duality does is exchange the rulers together with the spaces they measure. The bead of one description is the band of the other, and the circle it sees becomes the other circle.

This suggests reading the radius neither as hidden, nor as absent, nor as indeterminate, but as relative to the probe. Relative to beads the circle has one definite size, relative to bands it has another, and the theory states exactly how the two are related.

The structure has a precedent. Before 1905, whether two distant events are simultaneous looked like a fact about the events alone. Special relativity made simultaneity definite only relative to a frame of reference, with the Lorentz transformations relating the verdicts of different frames. Simultaneity did not become indeterminate or unreal; it became a relation. Here the probes play the role of the frames and T-duality that of the Lorentz transformations.

## The shortest length, reread

On this reading the shortest length acquires a simple meaning. When the circle is large, beads are light and bands are heavy; when it is small, bands are light and beads are heavy. The cheapest ruler, made of the lightest states, is the bead-ruler above $\ell_s$ and the band-ruler below it, and the size it reports is always the larger of $R$ and $\ell_s^2/R$:

$$
R_{\text{cheapest}} = \max\left(R,\ \frac{\ell_s^2}{R}\right) \geq \ell_s .
$$

The shortest length is therefore not the size of the smallest possible region of space. It is the smallest size the cheapest ruler can report, and it marks the point at which the two rulers cost the same and exchange roles. Anything built from light states, instruments included, sees a circle at least as large as $\ell_s$, whatever the value of $R$.

## More than a picture

The two rulers are not only a figure of speech. Freidel, Leigh and Minic have shown that the ordinary position on the circle and its winding counterpart do not commute, as position and momentum do not commute in quantum mechanics.[^6] A state can be sharp with respect to one of them, but not with respect to both. In their framework, choosing an ordinary spacetime amounts to choosing which of the two to treat as sharp, and T-duality is the change from one choice to the other. The reading proposed here adds one thing: the choice is made by the physical probe, not by the theorist.

## Limits

Everything above concerns closed strings at weak coupling, the regime in which T-duality is exact. String theory also contains heavier objects, D-branes, which can probe distances below $\ell_s$ and which see other geometries there.[^7] Whether the pairing between probes and the geometries they see survives in that regime is only partly understood, and it is the natural next test.

Nor does the argument depend on string theory being true. A coherent physical theory can make the size of a region depend on the kind of object that measures it, with a precise law relating the answers. If that is right, the lesson of T-duality is not that small distances are missing but that, at these scales, size is a relation.
