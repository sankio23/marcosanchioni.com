Niccolò Covoni and I have a new paper in the *European Journal for Philosophy of Science*, "Entanglement, relative facts, and the meaning of information in relational quantum mechanics". It is open access.[^paper]

Relational quantum mechanics, proposed by Carlo Rovelli in 1996, holds that the properties of a physical system have definite values only relative to another system with which it interacts. There is no privileged observer and no absolute state: an electron has a value of spin relative to the detector, the detector has a reading relative to the physicist, and each of these facts is relative to something. In this picture the notion of information plays a central role. Rovelli writes that any physical system may contain information about another, and gives the example of two spin-½ particles with the same value of spin in the same direction: one particle, he says, has information about the other.

The intuition is clear, but it is less clear what it amounts to once the observer has been removed from the picture. In ordinary use information is something that someone has (a receiver who reads a signal, an agent whose uncertainty is reduced), and this is also the setting of Shannon's theory, where information measures how reliably a message can be reproduced at a destination. If relational quantum mechanics wants to say that an electron has information about another electron, with nobody around, it needs a notion of information that does not presuppose a reader. Otherwise the observer the theory set out to remove comes back through the vocabulary.

Rovelli's later answer is formal. Two systems have information about each other when the number of joint states they can occupy is smaller than the product of the states each could occupy on its own. Information, in this sense, is correlation, i.e. the two systems are no longer independent. The definition is precise and has the right generality; however, it also covers correlations that have nothing specifically quantum or relational about them.

Take two dice joined by a rigid rod, so that whenever one shows six the other shows six as well. By the definition above, the dice have information about each other. Yet nothing in this correlation goes beyond the dice: once the properties of each die and the rod are fixed, the correlation is fully accounted for. Philosophers call a relation of this kind supervenient, since it is fixed by the properties of the things it relates. Such a correlation requires no relational ontology, and a classical physicist would describe it exactly as before.

Quantum mechanics has correlations of a different kind. Indeed, two particles can be in states that are locally identical, each with the same reduced state, and still differ in whether they are entangled. The entanglement is not written in either particle: it belongs to the pair, and it is not fixed by the properties of the relata. In the terminology of the paper, entanglement is an external, non-supervenient relation. This is a structural feature of quantum theory and holds on any interpretation.

Our thesis is that the information relevant to relational quantum mechanics is precisely this. To say that system A has information about system B is to say that A and B stand in an entanglement relation, which makes certain joint outcomes possible and others impossible for any future interaction between them. Quantities such as entanglement entropy or quantum mutual information do not define what information is; they measure how much of this relational structure is present.

Two facts about entanglement make the identification natural. The first is that entanglement does not arise by itself. If two systems evolve under a Hamiltonian that does not couple them, an initially uncorrelated state stays uncorrelated, so that entanglement requires a genuine interaction, and genuine interactions generically produce it (measurement is one case of this, with no special status). The second is that entanglement persists after the interaction is over. It is neither a signal nor an influence between distant systems; rather, it is a present constraint on what any future interaction between them can reveal.

Entanglement is therefore tied to interaction in both directions of time: it records a past coupling and structures the possible ones. This is the structure relational quantum mechanics needs. Its basic idea is that properties are attributed through interaction, relative to a system, and for that attribution to be non-arbitrary something physical has to fix which relative attributions are admissible. Entanglement plays this role and also meets a further requirement, since it does not depend on the choice of a basis or of a particular set of compatible observables, as an information measure defined on one set of variables does.

The place of entanglement in the theory is specific. Interactions, relative facts and the dynamics all remain part of the ontology of relational quantum mechanics, while entanglement is the structure that identifies which correlations can carry relational properties. Information, on this view, is physical without being a fundamental substance and without being knowledge: it is the entangled structure that constrains how the properties of systems can co-vary.

Back to Rovelli's two particles. If their spins agree because each was prepared that way, independently, they are like the dice: correlated, with nothing relational in the correlation. If they are entangled, the relation belongs to the pair and to neither particle alone, and in this second case the sentence "one particle has information about the other" says something that no description of the particles taken separately could say.
