The cube of physical theories has three axes: G, 1/c and ħ. Each vertex is a theory, obtained by switching the constants on or off.[^thermo] With all three off we have classical mechanics. G alone gives Newtonian gravity, 1/c alone special relativity, ħ alone quantum mechanics. With two constants on we get general relativity (G and 1/c), quantum field theory (1/c and ħ), and nonrelativistic quantum gravity (G and ħ). The vertex with all three is the theory we do not have. The picture is usually credited to Bronstein, who built on a paper by Gamow, Ivanenko and Landau.[^bronstein]

The constants do not vary, of course. Moving along an edge means taking the limit of a dimensionless ratio: v/c for velocities, GM/rc² for the strength of a gravitational field, ħ/S for the size of the quantum of action against the action of the system. "c goes to infinity" is shorthand for the first two becoming negligible.

With this in mind we can say where we are. At the surface of the Earth GM/rc² is about 7 × 10⁻¹⁰; at the horizon of a black hole it is one half. Everyday velocities give v/c around 10⁻⁷. For a macroscopic process with an action of one joule-second, ħ/S is 10⁻³⁴. We live in a very small neighbourhood of one vertex, the Newtonian one. Gravity there is weak by the relativistic measure, but it is dynamically relevant: things fall. Of the three regimes, gravity is the only one that shows up in ordinary experience; the effects of 1/c and ħ do not show up at all.

Our intuitions about what the world is made of were formed in that corner: bodies with positions and trajectories, a single time for everyone, a pull towards the ground. The question I am interested in is what happens to a picture of this kind, and more generally to the ontology of the theory at one vertex, once we know the theory at the next vertex along an edge.

Two theses should be kept apart. The weak one says that the more fundamental theory explains facts that are brute in the less fundamental one. This holds along practically every edge. General relativity explains why inertial and gravitational mass are equal. Quantum field theory explains the connection between spin and statistics, which nonrelativistic quantum mechanics has to postulate. Quantum mechanics explains the stability of matter. The strong thesis says more: that the more fundamental theory reformulates the less fundamental one inside its own domain, and so tells us what its objects were all along. Recovering the old theory in a limit is not enough for this. The revision has to be available without leaving the old regime.

Whether the strong thesis holds along a given edge depends, I think, on three conditions. The limit has to be regular rather than singular. There has to be a common formal framework in which both theories can be written. And the theory upstream has to have a determinate ontology, at least on the point in question.

The edge from general relativity to Newtonian gravity meets all three. Cartan, Friedrichs and Trautman showed that Newtonian gravity can be written as the curvature of a connection on a nonrelativistic spacetime, with no relativistic correction involved. Ehlers then placed the two theories in a single family, parametrised by 1/c², in which the Newtonian case is a regular limit.[^newton-cartan] In that formulation free fall is geodesic motion already in the Newtonian regime, and there is no gravitational force. The third condition is met well enough: debates on the nature of spacetime are open, but that gravity is curvature is not among the disputed points.[^teleparallel]

This edge is not the only one. Galilean spacetime, without absolute rest, is a reading of classical mechanics that became natural after Minkowski. Along the edge from general to special relativity, the Minkowski metric stops being a fixed background and becomes one configuration of a dynamical field. Along the edge from quantum mechanics to classical electromagnetism, the Aharonov–Bohm effect changed what we say about potentials in the classical theory too.[^ab]

So the gravitational edge is the cleanest case, and it does not differ in kind from the others. That it seems special has two sources, and it helps to separate them. One is structural: it satisfies the three conditions more fully than any other edge. The other is perspectival: it is the edge that starts from the vertex we inhabit, and it revises the one concept among the three that we meet in experience. The first is a matter of degree. The second is a fact about us.

The quantum edges are where the conditions usually fail. The limit in which ħ becomes negligible is singular, and quantum mechanics has no agreed ontology to pass down. Classical trajectories are not reformulated by it; they emerge, and how they emerge depends on the interpretation one adopts. The Aharonov–Bohm case is the exception, and an instructive one. The limit is singular here too, but the revision concerns a structure that both theories share, the connection of which the potential is a representative, and on this point quantum mechanics says the same thing under every interpretation. Along the edge from quantum field theory to nonrelativistic quantum mechanics the situation is mixed. For particles the strong thesis holds: indistinguishability, statistics and antiparticles all point to particles as excitations of fields, and this is a claim about the nonrelativistic regime as well. For the quantum state it does not, because field theory inherits the measurement problem unchanged. It has nothing determinate to say about the state that could be carried down the edge.

Whether field theory will eventually tell us what the nonrelativistic quantum state was all along is therefore open. The cube does not settle it. It does indicate what would: a formulation in which the two theories sit in one framework with a well-behaved limit, and an answer, upstream, to the question of what the state is.

[^thermo]: The cube is a heuristic and arguably an incomplete one. It has no place for thermodynamics: neither Boltzmann's constant, whose status as a fundamental constant is disputed, nor the number of degrees of freedom, whose limit N → ∞ is the textbook example of a singular limit. That the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy involves G, ħ, c and k_B together suggests the omission is not innocent.

See R. Aldrovandi, J. G. Pereira, *Teleparallel Gravity: An Introduction*, Springer, 2013; D.
